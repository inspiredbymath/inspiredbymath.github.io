## The Temptation

The first time I used an AI tool on a hard problem, I noticed something uncomfortable: it was very easy to ask for a full solution instead of a nudge, and to feel like I'd "solved" a problem I'd actually just read the solution to. That gap — between *understanding* a solution and *having found* it yourself — is easy to paper over with a chatbot.

## Rules I Try to Follow

1.  **Ask for a hint before an answer.** If I'm stuck, I'll ask an AI tool for the smallest possible nudge — a relevant theorem, a way to reframe the problem — before asking it to solve anything.
2.  **Never submit AI-generated work as my own thinking**, whether that's a competition writeup, a research proof, or a homework proof. If an AI helped me find an idea, I say so.
3.  **Use it to check, not to generate, for research.** AI tools are useful for catching algebra mistakes or suggesting a reference I might not know about, but the core argument has to be mine — partly because that's the only way I actually learn, and partly because AI models still confidently get proofs *wrong*.
4.  **Verify everything.** AI-generated math can look completely rigorous and still be subtly false — a miscounted case, an unjustified step, a "well-known" fact that isn't quite true. I treat every AI-suggested proof step the way I'd treat a claim from a classmate: plausible, not proven, until I check it myself.

## Where AI Genuinely Helps

*   **Explaining a concept a different way** when a textbook's phrasing isn't clicking.
*   **Generating extra practice problems** on a specific topic I'm weak in.
*   **Literature search** for research — pointing me toward papers or known results related to a question, which I then read myself.
*   **Catching typos and algebra slips** in long computations, the way a calculator would.

## Where It Doesn't (and Can Actively Hurt)

*   **Any time speed matters more than the tool's accuracy** — like getting a hint mid-competition-prep session and trusting it without checking.
*   **Any time the "productive struggle" is the point** — a lot of what makes competition math and research valuable is sitting with confusion long enough to build real intuition. Skipping that step with AI is a real cost, even if it doesn't feel like one in the moment.

## The Honest Summary

I think of AI the way I think of a very well-read, occasionally overconfident study partner: useful for talking through ideas, bad as a source of final answers, and never a substitute for actually doing the work myself. The goal isn't to avoid AI — it's to make sure it never replaces the parts of learning math that were the whole point in the first place.
